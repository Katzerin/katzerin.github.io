Musik

Recordings I love, and a few thoughts on them

Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber

Rosary Sonata No. 1 (Annunciation)

▶
Mateusz Kowalski (Guitar) Listen on YouTube

Biber’s Rosary Sonatas is my favourite baroque violin work, and to me it is no less important than Bach’s Sonatas and Partitas. This interpretation on guitar is well worth listening to. He recreates the beauty of the original on the guitar, stripped of the violin’s piercing edge. Sounds like the Virgin Mary’s caress.

Franz Schubert

4 Impromptus, D. 899 (Op. 90): No. 3 in G-Flat Major. Andante

▶
András Schiff (Fortepiano, ℗ 2019 ECM Records GmbH) Listen on YouTube

The left hand flows like a stream. Schubert loved evoking the sound of flowing water on the piano. Schiff’s tone on a fortepiano from Schubert’s time is exquisite.

Robert Schumann

Gesänge der Frühe, Op. 133: IV. Bewegt

▶
Maurizio Pollini (℗ 2002 Deutsche Grammophon GmbH, Berlin) Listen on YouTube

Schumann’s late piano piece—the last important one before he jumped into the Rhine. Compared to other famous interpretations like Ugorski’s—which is hauntingly slow—I love Pollini’s, which moves at a breathless, almost flying pace. This movement is filled with the restlessness and neurotic tension of Schumann on the brink of mental collapse. Its relentless syncopation is like a racing, off-balance heartbeat; a sudden step into thin air. I synchronized with this tempo during a specific chapter of my life.

Johann Sebastian Bach

Partita No. 2 in D Minor, BWV 1004: V. Chaconne

▶
Nathan Milstein (1986 Farewell Performance) Listen on YouTube

Milstein’s 1986 farewell performance of Bach’s Chaconne. You can clearly hear the technical imperfections, and it’s somehow cruel, for it indicates the senescence of the artist’s flesh. It’s Milstein at the age of 82; his left hand had suffered a fracture, and his stamina was declining. Yet, you can feel such a powerful sense of human dignity within it. How he confronts aging and death, standing there, striving to deliver his lifelong artistry and contemplation, with his whole heart. It’s the perfect finale I could imagine anyone giving at the end of his life—not only presented to the audience, but to God, to himself. I can hardly listen to this version because the emotional weight is simply overwhelming.

Johann Sebastian Bach

Partita No. 1 for Solo Violin in B Minor, BWV 1002: I. Allemanda

▶
Gidon Kremer (℗ 2005 ECM Records GmbH) Listen on YouTube

When I first heard this piece on radio at the age of 12, I fell for it instantly. It was recorded in a chapel, giving it a sense of space. And Kremer’s voice has a distinct astringency.

Franz Schubert

Schwanengesang, D. 957 - Am Meer

▶
Matthias Goerne (℗ harmonia mundi France) Listen on YouTube

It’s a rather lesser-known Schubert Lied. It reminds me of the mermaid tale in Turgenev’s Bezhin Meadow. Both capture a trauma: once a mortal briefly touches an otherworldly beauty, the body may escape, but the soul is never at peace again.

Robert Schumann

8 Fantasiestücke, Op. 12: 5. In der Nacht

▶
Friedrich Gulda (℗ 1984 Universal International Music B.V.) Listen on YouTube

In his early works, Schumann already showed this manic neurosis. Yet at the F-major turn in the middle section, the tone has to pivot instantly from turbulence to utterly tender cantabile. Gulda’s humor adds a great touch.

Johannes Brahms

Piano Trio No. 1 in B, Op. 8: 1. Allegro con brio

▶
Beaux Arts Trio (℗ 1987 Universal International Music B.V.) Listen on YouTube

I deeply love ALL of Brahms’s chamber music. Full of flowing vitality and rich harmony. Of them all, Op.8 holds the purest spirit of youth.
